## Lightning talk (sessions 1 & 2 )

**BENTH-MARC: A new database for benthos in the marine Arctic, with a user perspective on OBIS data** *Presented by Hanna Modin (UiT / The Arctic University of Norway / OBIS Norway)*

Hanna Modin presented the early stages of BENTH-MARC, a new database compiling publicly available zoobenthic biodiversity records across the Arctic, developed in the framework of POMP (Polar Ocean Mitigation Potential), an EU project focused on biodiversity and carbon in the polar regions. Hanna Modin is not formally part of OBIS Norway but has worked extensively with OBIS data.

The workflow harvests all benthic phyla from within the Arctic Large Marine Ecosystems (PAME 2014) from OBIS, CritterBase, GBIF, OGSL, and a set of unpublished datasets, then filters out pelagic, terrestrial, and extinct taxa, cleans, harmonizes, and merges the data. The current version holds around 10,354 taxa, 3.6 million records, and 134,192 stations. Hanna highlighted four OBIS-specific issues encountered while preparing the database, framed as constructive suggestions:

**\> Only about 58% of occurrence records returned a populated datasetName field.**The names can be retrieved manually from the OBIS website using the dataset\_id, but the automatic fetch appears broken or inconsistent, which becomes a significant constraint when working with large numbers of datasets. As a result, she has tended to prefer GBIF data wherever duplicates exist between the two infrastructures.

**\> Conditional taxonomic downloads currently lose NaN entries at the relevant taxonomic level.**This forces the user to first download all records belonging to a given group and then filter locally. For taxa such as Porifera and Bryozoa, where the highest available taxonomic resolution is often only at the phylum level, retaining NaN entries is essential.

**\> The eventDate column seems heterogeneous, with a wide range of formats, and even some dates set very far back in time or in the future.**

\> Building on the existing environment information already added from WoRMS (which she described as very useful), **Hanna suggested exposing functional group information (benthic/pelagic) directly in OBIS data at harvest**, since this is programmatically accessible from WoRMS and currently covers approximately 80% of accepted, extant marine species.

→ **Pieter Provoost** (OBIS Secretariat) addressed two of the issues during the second session.\> **On datasetName,** he clarified that the field is only populated if the data provider populates it for every occurrence record; however, the dataset title is always available via the dataset metadata, and can be retrieved separately via a dedicated function in the robis R package and joined to the occurrence records.

\> **On eventDate**, he explained that OBIS keeps the original (sometimes messy) value as provided, but adds a number of interpreted fields (date\_year, start, mid, and end timestamps as numeric values) precisely to make downstream parsing easier in R, Python, or Excel. He invited further discussion on whether users would prefer an additional ISO-formatted output field.

→ **Hanieh Saeedi** (OBIS Deep Sea) asked what proportion of the BENTH-MARC records are deep-sea data, in order to compare with her own deep-sea benthos compilation. Hanna Modin offered to share the breakdown after the meeting.

Node members are encouraged to contact Hanna Modin for collaborations or further discussion at hanna.m.modin@uit.no

## Insights from BBNJ PrepCom3Presented by Ward Appeltans, Head of the OBIS SecretariatWard Appeltans reported back from the third Preparatory Commission meeting (PrepCom3) for the BBNJ Agreement, held in New York from 23 March to 2 April 2026. The BBNJ Agreement entered into force on 17 January 2026, which means the first Conference of the Parties (COP-1) will be organized within one year and is now scheduled in New York from 11 to 22 January 2027. To date, 88 countries have ratified the Agreement.

**What was decided**Discussions at PrepCom3 focused heavily on the Clearing-House Mechanism (CHM), the digital backbone of the Agreement: without it, key obligations (six-month advance notification of cruises in areas beyond national jurisdiction, use of standardized batch identifiers for samples, etc.) cannot be implemented. Ward highlighted that the CHM itself is not expected to host the underlying data: the CHM would store metadata pointing back to existing resources.PrepCom3 ended with the adoption of an [Outcome Document](https://www.un.org/bbnjagreement/sites/default/files/2026-04/CoFacilitators_CHM_2April.pdf)**,** setting a clear path toward an operational prototype of the CHM before COP-1. Development will be guided by the BNNJ Agreement Co-Chairs, the Bureau, and DOALOS. An open call for expressions of interest will be issued, inviting the four organizations referenced in Article 51 (IOC, IMO, FAO, and the ISA), as well as anyone interested, to make a proposal that must include a detailed work plan and costings. The selected technical partner will operate under the oversight of the Co-Chairs, Bureau, and DOALOS, will be required to connect and consult with relevant groups such as national focal points, non-Party users, and Indigenous Peoples and local communities, with an obligation to deliver by COP-1. Continuation will depend on the COP assessment of the prototype.

**Priority functions for the prototype**The Outcome Document identifies four priority functions to be delivered before COP-1:(a) MGR notifications and the BBNJ standardized batch identifier;(b) match-making for capacity-building and transfer of marine technology (CB/TMT), for example, connecting people from developing countries to berths and vessels;(c) notifications related to environmental impact assessments (EIAs);(d) basic infrastructure for area-based management tools (ABMTs), with some countries already pushing for a geospatial interface around marine protected areas.Ward noted that the list is ambitious, with a short timeline: the prototype, including iterative consultations, needs to be ready before the end of the year.

**Relevance for OBIS**Several technical parameters in the Outcome Document map directly onto the OBIS network:

metadata standards interoperable with national, sub-regional, regional, and global databases (parameters a, f);

a central system designed for future integration of nodes, in line with the distributed OBIS architecture (parameter g);

support for low-bandwidth environments and offline submission (parameter j);

capacity-building components integrated into the prototype, connecting to OceanTeacher and OBIS training activities (paragraph 10);

and a prototype in English with the final system in all six UN languages (parameter h).

Ward also highlighted a strong push to design the CHM as a federated system with regional or national nodes rather than a purely central system. Countries see the future BBNJ nodes as places that would also offer helpdesk and support functions to assist governments and researchers with EIA or MPA proposals, or with compliance on marine genetic resources. The OBIS Nodes network is being looked at as a potential answer to that need.**Key messages and next steps**OBIS is following the BBNJ process closely and is ready to support the development of the prototype if IOC is selected. The call is expected within weeks, with a probable submission deadline at the end of May. The IOC Executive Council, in late June, would still need to welcome the IOC’s proposed expression of interest. If IOC is selected, the OBIS Secretariat will likely lead the work, with significant involvement from Pieter Provoost, Steve Formel, Silas Principe, and others, from approximately July through to January 2027. Additional staff will need to be hired for both development and consultation, which will inevitably draw some resources away from "core" OBIS business in the second half of the year.→ **Kit Elloran** (Sea-OBIS) asked about the minimum data standards and interoperability protocols needed to align OBIS datasets with BBNJ reporting, especially for deep-sea and high-seas biodiversity. Ward Appeltans clarified that the CHM is not expected to host the underlying datasets but to keep the necessary metadata pointing to the data sources, for example, via DOIs, with the data themselves remaining in OBIS, GBIF, or other databases. The minimum metadata set has not yet been identified and will need to be defined as part of the prototype development.→ **Katherine Tattersall** (OBIS Australia, OBIS SG Co-Chair) shared a concrete experience from the Australian Node: the Australian delegation involved in the BBNJ negotiations had a strong legal and policy background but limited expertise in biodiversity data architecture, and approached the Node for advice. She encouraged other Nodes to proactively send a short message to their national delegations to flag their expertise, even if they do not feel "ready" to engage, as this can open the door for further discussions.

## Communication kit for the OBIS Nodes

*Presented by Laurent Chmiel, OBIS Secretariat*

Laurent Chmiel gave an update on Deliverable 2.1 of the OBIS work plan: a building-block-based communication toolkit aimed at funders, backers, and host organizations, that can be adapted to each OBIS Node. The starting point is the recognition that OBIS Nodes operate in very different institutional contexts, with different resources and challenges. The approach to building the kit is structured around five steps:

Each Node identifies up to ten of its key stakeholders, where a "stakeholder" can be a group, a person (e.g., the dean of a hosting institution), or an authority.

Each Node fills in a simple, table-based template that qualifies each stakeholder: who they are, how much influence and interest they have, what the Node expects from them, what they expect from the Node, and what the Node wants to achieve through communication with them.

The Secretariat builds a typology of stakeholder profiles.

The Secretariat builds communication blocks to support Nodes’ communication actions and objectives.

Nodes pick and choose from these blocks to fit their needs and produce their own assets. A typical use case: a Node manager hosted in a university with a ten-minute slot once a year to argue for continued or increased investment from the dean, with the kit doing the legwork on key messaging.

The template will be sent to Nodes around 6 May 2026, with a deadline for completion of 6 June 2026. Materials will initially be in English, with the intention to localize them over time; for Spanish-speaking Nodes, the template can already be sent in Spanish from the start. The Secretariat and the NCG Co-Chairs are available to support Nodes throughout the process. Further details will be shared in the next OBIS Pulse newsletter and by email.→ **Ben Donnelly** (OBIS-SEAMAP) asked for a concrete example of what a "communication block" would look like in practice. Laurent Chmiel illustrated this with the dean / university scenario above, noting that the difficulty of the exercise lies in the diversity of Nodes (financial, institutional, administrative). The first iteration will already be ambitious; subsequent iterations can be expanded year on year, for example, with more targeted strategies for specific actions, such as approaching a ministry for additional budget.
