## Introduction

NCG Co-Chairs John Nicholls (first session) and Maria Cornthwaite (second session) welcomed participants to the thirteenth meeting of the OBIS Nodes Coordination Group. The meeting was a special edition: both Co-Chairs are completing their terms of office and stepping down after SG-OBIS-14, and both are retiring from their positions. The agenda combined a presentation from the OBIS Deep Sea Node, reflections from the two outgoing Co-Chairs, an open discussion on the future of the OBIS Nodes Awards, and updates from the coordination groups, the Steering Group and the Secretariat.

## OBIS Deep Sea: a decade of mobilizing deep-ocean biodiversity data

**Presented by Hanieh Saeedi, OBIS Deep Sea Node (session 2)**

The OBIS Deep Sea Node was established in 2016, following a workshop that brought deep-sea scientists together to address the need for improved sharing, integration and accessibility of deep-sea biodiversity data. It is one of the OBIS thematic nodes, hosted by the OBIS Secretariat in Oostende and managed by Hanieh Saeedi at the Senckenberg Research Institute and Natural History Museums. The Node operates as a community effort, with support from a range of initiatives: DOOS, the Deep Ocean Observing Strategy, a GOOS project recently endorsed as a UN Ocean Decade programme; the INDEEP project, established in 2011; and Challenger 150, also an endorsed Ocean Decade programme, which coordinates deep-sea expeditions worldwide and mobilizes the resulting data.

**The data.** The Node currently holds over 300,000 occurrence records covering more than 3,000 unique deep-sea species. Given how understudied the deep sea remains, despite being one of the largest ecosystems on Earth, this is a substantial figure. A significant early contribution came from the ten-year Russian-German expedition to the Kuril-Kamchatka Trench led by Angelika Brandt, which Hanieh Saeedi was appointed in 2017 to mobilize into OBIS, and which remains visible as a dense cluster on the Node’s distribution map. Starting from only a few thousand occurrence records in 2017, the Node has grown steadily through unique contributions. Considerably more deep-sea data sits in other OBIS nodes.

**Activities.** Alongside the standard Node work of publication support, quality control, vocabulary alignment and formatting assistance for data providers, Senckenberg is an active natural history collection with a strong deep-sea research programme. The Node mobilizes unique museum collections, gathered in collaboration with seagoing institutes, into both OBIS and GBIF.

**Training.** The most recent capacity development activity was a workshop coordinated by DOOS. Of around 30 applications, participants were selected on two criteria: early-career status, and holding a dataset they actually intended to publish, so that the training would not be theoretical. Participants worked hands-on with their own data, working through the real challenges of aligning datasets with Darwin Core, and received advanced Darwin Core data package training with a view to contributing to the OBIS Deep Sea Node and to other appropriate nodes, including US OBIS. They also learned to access and reuse data already in OBIS, with data visualization and extraction taught in R and in Python. The group built connections among themselves and became a growing community of practice for standardizing and mobilizing deep-ocean biodiversity data, and shared expertise with other accelerator teams, including work feeding into emerging BBNJ guidance. Of the eleven datasets involved, five were already close to completion shortly after the workshop, with the remainder in preparation.

**Wider collaboration.** The Node works to grow the next generation of data users and providers, with courses designed so that bachelor and master students use biodiversity data from OBIS and GBIF and learn why quality control and data sharing matter for conservation, management and decision-making. International collaborations include DiSSCo, the Distributed System of Scientific Collections, which is building infrastructure to mobilize collection data into open databases; the second phase of TETTRIs, a Horizon Europe project developing a next-generation European taxonomic system along with the tools, services and infrastructure to make biodiversity data available; and NFDI4Biodiversity, the German national infrastructure for open biodiversity data. The Node also supports biodiversity assessment and conservation, and was represented in the invasive alien species assessment.

→ Ruben Perez Perez (OBIS DCG Co-Chair) asked how participants are kept engaged after a workshop ends. Hanieh Saeedi explained that the key was front-loading the effort: she and her co-trainer held individual meetings with participants well before the workshop, asking them to bring their dataset and their questions, so that most datasets were already well advanced by the time the workshop began. Follow-up has continued directly with the data providers since.

→ Ben Donnelly asked whether the Node interacts with the International Seabed Authority, and whether ISA deep-sea mining processes generate requests for particular data reports. Hanieh Saeedi noted the substantial overlap and collaboration between the two nodes. Luciana Genio (OBIS ISA) added that the ISA Node’s data providers are quite different, coming through exploration contractors and covering only areas beyond national jurisdiction, which makes the two nodes complementary, since the deep sea extends both within and beyond national jurisdiction. She saw clear scope for doing more together on data mobilization.

→ Luciana Genio (OBIS ISA) also asked how the impact of in-person data mobilization workshops compares with online training, and whether the efficiency of the two approaches can be measured. Hanieh Saeedi replied that the workshop had been intended to be fully in person, but that she, as a trainer, was unable to obtain a visa, as were other participants from the Global South, so she attended virtually while the participants met in person. The in-person format worked well: participants brainstormed together, helped each other, and the more experienced supported those with less experience, which would have been hard to replicate online. Luciana Genio noted that this is a useful case to make when mobilizing resources for future training.

→ Katherine Tattersall (OBIS Australia, OBIS SG Co-Chair) described an enquiry from an Australian publisher who asked whether deep-sea datasets now had to be split out and published through OBIS Deep Sea, separately from their shallow-water data, and asked what general advice applies where a thematic node and a national node overlap. Hanieh Saeedi agreed datasets should not be split: species distribution ranges are ecologically continuous, users often search for specific dataset names, and splitting diminishes the value of the full range. What matters is that the data reaches OBIS, not which node it comes through. On guidance, she pointed to the OBIS manual as complete and sufficient, since Darwin Core terms do not differ between shallow-water and deep-sea data, and offered her own expertise for questions on the ecological or biological background behind particular terms.

→ Clara Baringo (SiBBr, OBIS Brazil) asked whether there are specific good practices or terms to pass on to publishers, and how to indicate that data published through a national IPT is deep-sea data. Hanieh Saeedi explained that the definition depends on the depth threshold used: the World Register of Deep-Sea Species uses below 500 metres, while traditional textbooks use below 200 metres. She recommended below 200 metres, which can be applied as a depth filter in the OBIS mapper or through the R package to retrieve deep-sea data across all nodes.

## Reflections from the outgoing NCG Co-Chairs

**John Nicholls, OBIS OPI, and Maria Cornthwaite, OBIS Canada**

John Nicholls is a data manager and researcher at Trinity College Dublin, working with the Trinity Centre for Environmental Humanities, and manages the OBIS OPI Node for the Oceans Past Initiative. He opened by setting out a deliberately varied background, from civil engineering, systems analysis and a master’s in philosophy of mind through to Gestalt psychotherapy and athletics coaching, alongside earlier work as a police officer, a lumberjack for the Forestry Commission in Scotland, a highways officer in England, a technical author at Philips Medical Systems in the Netherlands, and a data manager for the History of Marine Animal Populations project, which was present at the origins of OBIS. The point of the list was that a Co-Chair does not need one particular profile: the role calls for someone willing to take on different tasks and to recognize and work with people from very different backgrounds.

**On the challenges.** Funding has always been tight, OBIS operates alongside other data networks and has had to work continually to hold and improve its position, and change has been the one constant, with structural decisions arriving from the various layers of hierarchy within UNESCO. These pressures have been demanding, yet they have also shaped a community that has had to hold together.

**On the rewards.** Most of the experience has been positive: sharing with different nodes, building connections inside and outside OBIS, and gaining a detailed understanding of how OBIS is managed, particularly through the seat on the Executive Committee that comes with the role. John Nicholls thanked Laurent Chmiel for the work behind the NCG, the Secretariat for consistent support, Steering Group Co-Chairs Katherine Tattersall and Dan Lear for their volunteer effort, the Data and Products Coordination Group Co-Chairs for their steady engagement, and the Node managers and staff who do the underlying work. He closed on the theme that ran through both sessions: OBIS functions as a family of people from very different disciplines, and no Node should consider itself a distant relation.

Maria Cornthwaite manages OBIS Canada from the Pacific Biological Station, where she began as a student in the 1990s, working first as a research technician in the groundfish section and taking over as manager of the Groundfish Data Unit in 2016. She joined OBIS in 2021, taking over the Node from Bruce Patton, and became NCG Co-Chair after SG-OBIS-12, in part because she had asked John Nicholls why meetings were not yet running and whether dual sessions could be arranged so that colleagues in her time zone would not have to attend in the middle of the night. She is a firm believer in data managers going to sea to see where their data comes from, and has taken part in a range of vessel-based surveys, some of which are published in OBIS.

**On taking the role.** Coming from a Node that operates without dedicated resources or staff, Maria Cornthwaite had always felt limited in what she could commit to, but found that being Co-Chair was a way to contribute meaningfully without a heavy time cost, and her experience bore that out. She encouraged Nodes considering the role to remember that each person brings their own perspective and skills: in her case a Pacific time zone, a pragmatic view of what a small but capable Node can accomplish, and considerable prior experience of chairing meetings. In return, a Co-Chair becomes part of something larger than their own Node, joins the OBIS Executive Committee and helps guide the work plan and the direction of OBIS, and can take those accomplishments back to their host organization. She was clear that the accomplishments belong to the group: the Nodes determine what is possible and build the work plan, while the Co-Chairs guide the process and keep the meetings running.

Both Co-Chairs thanked Laurent Chmiel for carrying much of the work behind the group, and each other for the partnership. Maria Cornthwaite noted that Peter Kraska, who joined OBIS Canada recently from St Andrews in New Brunswick, will take over the Node when she retires in the coming months. John Nicholls added that what he valued most from in-person OBIS gatherings were the conversations after the formal sessions, where friendships were built.

→ Ward Appeltans (Head of OBIS Secretariat) responded that what drives the community is the community itself, and that the energy to continue through the challenges and frustrations comes from the people in these meetings rather than from working alone at a computer. He highlighted that the strength of OBIS is that everyone behaves as a leader and takes responsibility, with nobody feeling that they do not matter, and thanked those willing to step up as Co-Chairs for taking on that additional responsibility in a global network with limited funding. He noted that past Co-Chairs are not forgotten and should be properly recognized for their service.

## Open discussion: the future of the OBIS Nodes Awards

Following the awards handed out at the last Steering Group meeting, which were well received, both sessions discussed whether to continue the OBIS Nodes Awards and how they should be organized. There was no opposition to continuing. The discussion turned on three questions: who should be eligible, what form the award should take, and who should select the recipients.

**Eligibility.** There was broad support for widening the awards beyond Node staff. Ruben Perez Perez supported recognizing effort at a personal level and proposed including members of the Executive Committee, who were excluded last time because they were the ones voting, suggesting that the voting mechanism could be arranged differently to make this possible. Laurent Chmiel proposed opening the awards to the data providers that Nodes work with daily, on the grounds that the entire data architecture rests on them, and extending recognition to supporting institutions as well as individuals. In the second session he set out a concrete model of two streams: each Node nominating one data provider for recognition that year, alongside a smaller number of nominations for Node members who have done outstanding work, which would produce a large but representative set of nominees.

**Criteria.** Martha Vides (OBIS Colombia) supported recognizing data providers but asked that the categories be defined clearly before nominations open, so that everyone understands what they are nominating for. She questioned whether data provider recognition should rest on volume of data or on the significance of the effort, giving the example of a new Node such as Panama preparing to publish its first dataset, where a single contribution represents a significant step in growing the network. Laurent Chmiel agreed that effort rather than quantity should be the basis, and asked Nodes to send any further suggestions quickly, since the matter needs to move fast.

**Form of the award.** John Nicholls noted that the Secretariat is not in a position to fund the kind of prize recipients deserve, and proposed certificates together with recognition on the website and in OBIS correspondence, with recipients able to print their own certificate. Laurent Chmiel supported a permanent presence on the website and raised the possibility of blockchain-based certificates, noting that this may require a third party and therefore a budget. Physical awards are likely to remain feasible for Node members, but not for data providers and institutions, where distribution would be a logistical problem.

**Selection.** Maria Cornthwaite asked who would choose the recipients, noting that last time the decision was taken among the Co-Chairs, and asked whether a small committee drawn from the Nodes is needed if the awards are widened. Katherine Tattersall confirmed that the Executive Committee had effectively made the decision last time, and argued that a call to Nodes for nominations of data providers is the only fair and effective approach, since it is the Nodes who know their data providers best.

**Next steps.** The process was not settled in the meeting. Suggestions from both sessions will be gathered and a mechanism, together with clear categories, will be developed quickly, ahead of the Steering Group meeting. Nodes are invited to send suggestions to the Secretariat, and to indicate whether they would like to take part in the selection. Once the mechanism is agreed, a note will be included in the OBIS Pulse newsletter inviting nominations.

## Data Coordination Group update

**Presented by Ruben Perez Perez, DCG Co-Chair (both sessions)**

**Co-Chair vacancy.** Ruben Perez Perez is leaving the role of DCG Co-Chair, while Victor Chocho continues. Nominations, including self-nominations, are welcome from Nodes interested in organizing the group and overseeing implementation of the work plan. Interested candidates can contact the OBIS helpdesk.

**Essential Ocean Variables.** The implementation plan has been written, reviewed, approved and published, setting out the way forward for tagging datasets with EOVs in a semi-automated way. The automation itself still needs to be developed. In the meantime the group has carried out a first round of manual tagging, and the results are already visible: an example shown from the BioEco portal displayed a dataset tagged as belonging to the seagrass cover and composition EOV and to marine turtles abundance and distribution.

**Workshops.** The eDNA workshop held in July is available as a recording for anyone who missed it. The Darwin Core data package workshop planned for this year has been delayed, owing to delays on the GBIF Secretariat side, and will not take place before the Steering Group meeting. The group is working out how to deliver it later.

**eDNA training.** The eDNA subgroup has been building a wish list for next year’s work plan. Two themes have emerged: how the Darwin Core data package and environmental DNA data will fit together, so that clear guidance can be given to publishers and data providers, and the development of more advanced training materials building on the course infrastructure already in place. One candidate topic is eDNA for species distribution modelling, for which no trainer has yet been identified: anyone with experience in this area, or who can suggest someone, is asked to get in touch. The eDNA working group under the DCG is open to Nodes with questions on the subject.

**Taxon annotations.** The internal WoRMS non-matching taxa validation training and annotation sprint took place, bringing together around ten participants from three different countries to work on validating and resolving non-matching taxonomic records in OBIS together with WoRMS. The workshop itself is complete, and the work will continue through the year to improve the quality of taxonomic annotation across OBIS and WoRMS.

## Products Coordination Group update

**Presented by Katherine Tattersall (session 1) and Jonathan Pye, PCG Co-Chair (session 2)**

**Governance and submission guidelines.** The PCG held its meetings shortly before the NCG, with Silas Principe leading the first session and Pieter Provoost attending the second. The main topic was the submission guidelines and governance for the products catalogue, which have been open for review within the group. They cover which kinds of products can and should be published and how those decisions will be made. The guidelines will be ratified at SG-OBIS-14 in Oostende.

**The products catalogue.** The catalogue at products.obis.org is in a firming soft launch. It is built on a CKAN instance, which will allow it to be connected to systems such as ODIS in future. It currently holds a small set of key products developed by the OBIS Secretariat, which serve as examples, and the next step for the PCG is to begin publishing products developed by OBIS Nodes. The range is broad, from derived data products through to models, and the group discussed how to tag and mark up product types so that users can filter and find what they need.

**New visuals for Node pages.** The Secretariat, with advice from Jonathan Pye and Steve Formel and with much of the work done by Pieter Provoost, demonstrated a prototype of summary statistics for each Node, sitting on the OBIS Node pages. The views included records over time on a per-Node basis, and temporal and taxonomic views at both Node and dataset level, including seasonal observation plots. The demonstration was received with enthusiasm, on the basis that Nodes frequently need exactly this kind of material to present and promote their work.

**SG-OBIS-14.** A hands-on session is planned at the Steering Group meeting, where Nodes will work through the data products and views they need and do not yet have, covering the full range of their contributions to OBIS. Nodes are encouraged to come prepared with ideas.

## Steering Group update

**Presented by Katherine Tattersall (session 1) and Dan Lear (session 2), OBIS SG Co-Chairs**

**GBIF Governing Board.** Dan Lear attended the GBIF Governing Board meeting in Oslo as the OBIS observer. OBIS was recognized throughout: the eDNA collaboration in the joint work plan was shown on screen alongside the OBIS logo, and Tim Robertson of GBIF credited OBIS, highlighting SCAR MarBIN in particular for its contribution of survey and monitoring data. The New Zealand delegation cited OBIS engagement through the GBIF BID programme. As formal interventions were not possible, Dan Lear submitted a written statement congratulating GBIF on its 25th anniversary and reflecting on the joint collaboration and the growing number of nodes shared across both networks.

**Pre-SG work plan meetings.** Thanks were extended to all the Nodes and coordination groups that took part in the online pre-Steering Group meetings. An online meeting is not the same as meeting face to face, but the process produced a thorough work plan that had been run past the Nodes and endorsed by the Steering Group before being submitted, which the Co-Chairs considered essential.

**IODE-29.** The IODE-29 committee meeting was held the previous week, across seven sessions over five days. The committee welcomed the OBIS work plan and budget and will submit it to the IOC Secretariat for adoption at the IOC Assembly, after which it is expected to be adopted at the UNESCO General Conference, bringing the 2028-2029 work plan into effect.

**Katherine Tattersall elected IODE Co-Chair.** Katherine Tattersall was elected Co-Chair of IODE, and will serve alongside Alessandra Giorgetti of Italy, succeeding the outgoing Co-Chairs who have completed their two-term appointments. She described the decision to stand as a difficult one, given how much the OBIS community means to her, but saw important work to be done in the role. Dan Lear welcomed the outcome as good news for OBIS, which gains a strong advocate who understands the community, and Ward Appeltans noted that the two roles cannot normally be held at once. Katherine Tattersall will therefore transition out of the OBIS Steering Group Co-Chair role on a timeline yet to be defined, with an expression of interest for Steering Group Co-Chairs to be issued in the near future and details shared with the whole community. She will remain within the Executive Committee as past Co-Chair, and continues as OBIS Australia Node manager.

**SG-OBIS-14.** The Steering Group meeting takes place in Oostende from 11 to 16 November 2026, back-to-back with the World Conference on Marine Biodiversity in Bruges. The agenda has been circulated and is available on OceanExpert. The meeting will cover Node reports, training, workshops run by the coordination groups, enrichment of the endorsed work plan, and the nomination and election of coordination group Co-Chairs for the vacant roles. Nodes with a particular topic, question or success they wish to raise are asked to contact the Co-Chairs in advance so that appropriate time can be allocated in the agenda.

## Secretariat update

**Presented by Ward Appeltans (session 1) and Laurent Chmiel (session 2)**

**GBIF Ebbe Nielsen Challenge.** Two of the four awards in this year’s GBIF Ebbe Nielsen Challenge went to OBIS-linked work. One was the OBIS species distribution models, developed by Silas Principe with Pieter Provoost and Ward Appeltans. Ward Appeltans noted that Silas Principe, who built the models, chose to share the award with colleagues. An article about the awards is available on the OBIS website.

**BBNJ webinar series.** A webinar series on the BBNJ clearing-house mechanism, organized by the High Seas Alliance, opens on 15 October. It is an information session rather than a hands-on workshop, and is useful background for understanding what the clearing-house mechanism involves and what is at stake. Registration closes shortly. OBIS is advocating for a role for OBIS Nodes in connecting to the clearing-house mechanism. The series is run by a non-governmental organization alliance rather than by member states.

**eDNA Expeditions.** The project is running at full speed, with sampling under way at sites around the world. The data management platform, developed largely by Pieter Provoost, is now operational: sites register their samples, samples are sent to the laboratory, the sequences are returned and run through the bioinformatics and quality control pipeline, and Darwin Core output is produced. The platform also provides an interface where scientists can review the taxonomic annotations coming out of the pipeline and accept or reject them, choose which bioinformatics pipeline to run, and identify or upload their own reference libraries. The ambition is to open it to other OBIS Nodes for their own projects in future, both as an open platform and as a way for Nodes and the Secretariat to compare and learn from each other’s pipelines.

A press event organized by UNESCO to present the project took place on 15 October, and eDNA Expeditions will feature virtually at CBD COP17, where UNESCO is presenting the project as one of its highlights under the CBD. A call is open for sampling sites to express interest in joining the project: 25 sites will receive sampling kits and support to run eDNA sampling locally. Because sites are expected to carry out six to nine sampling campaigns, which is a significant commitment, the intention is to build on existing monitoring programmes rather than establish new ones, and one of the criteria in the survey is whether the site works with an OBIS Node. The call is open until mid-February. Additional funding may allow expansion to further sites.

**Connecting the project to the Nodes.** The Secretariat was emphatic that eDNA Expeditions should be a community project rather than a Secretariat project. Several Nodes are already heavily involved, including OBIS Japan in the sampling in Okinawa, as well as Colombia and Malaysia. The Secretariat will contact Nodes directly with suggestions for how they might be involved where sampling is taking place in their region, with no obligation attached. Nodes that know of suitable sites, ideally but not necessarily protected areas, are encouraged to get in touch.

**SG-OBIS-14 participation.** As of the meeting, 17 Nodes were expected in person and 10 online, with a number still pending travel funding or visa approval, four unable to participate, and several yet to respond. Ward Appeltans encouraged outstanding Nodes to confirm.
